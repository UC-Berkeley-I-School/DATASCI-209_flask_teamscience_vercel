Disrupted NIH Grants

Intro

The National Institutes of Health (NIH) is the largest public funder of biomedical and behavioral research in the world. As part of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, the NIH invests tens of billions of dollars annually to support research conducted at universities, hospitals, and research institutions across the United States.

The NIH is an entirely government-funded institution whose history traces back to 1887 (NIH, 2025a). The NIH funds projects spanning areas such as cancer, infectious diseases, neuroscience, mental health, public health, and emerging biomedical technologies. These grants support scientific discovery, clinical research, and the development of new treatments that improve public health. Here are some of their most important discoveries:

  • Cracked the genetic code
  • Led the public Human Genome Project (NIH, 2025b)
  • Ran the studies that established fluoride prevents tooth decay (NIH, 2024)
  • Helped discover mRNA vaccine technology
  • Funded the basic science behind gene therapy, gene editing over decades (NIH, 2025b).

The NIH was involved with 354 out of 356 drugs approved by the FDA in 2010 to 2019 (99.4%)

How NIH Grant Funding Works

NIH grants typically follow a structured lifecycle. Researchers submit proposals, which undergo rigorous peer review before funding decisions are made. Once awarded, grants receive funding over multiple years as research milestones are completed. Under normal circumstances, projects continue until their planned completion date.

The disruption events analyzed in this project represent departures from this normal funding process. Grants may be frozen, terminated, reinstated, or restored after review, creating interruptions to ongoing research activities. The visualizations below explore when these disruptions occurred, how frequently grants were restored, and the magnitude of funding affected.

NIH Grant Terminations Were Historically Rare Until 2025

From 2012 through January 20, 2025, NIH terminated fewer than six grants midstream. Beginning in 2025, thousands of grant disruption events were recorded within a matter of months.

Wasted Time and Money

In 2025, NIH terminated 1,249 research grants and did not restore them. This section asks the question: How much time and money was spent on disrupted projects?

5.1 Where the money stood when the work stopped — 815 grants

Every dollar NIH commits to a grant ends up in one of three places once the grant is cut: already spent, still sitting there, or formally taken back.

$1.09 billion was committed to these grants. $620 million of it had already been paid out for work that then stopped and that money is gone regardless of what happens next. The government formally reclaimed $151 million, about 14 cents of every dollar it had committed.

What this chart can't say. It covers 815 of the 1,249 terminated grants (the ones with spending figures on record). The other 434 have none at all, and they aren't a random sample: they had been funded for a median of 5 years, against 2 years for the grants shown here. Older grants have spent more, so the real total is higher than $1.09 billion, not lower.

On 294 grants, NIH stopped the work and then never reclaimed the money. $182.6 million remains formally committed to research that isn't happening (neither spent nor recovered).

5.2 Most were cut mid-stream (1,126 grants)

A grant has a scheduled start and end. This chart asks how far through that schedule each grant had gotten when it was terminated.

What it says. Half the grants that were cut were more than 59% of the way through their scheduled work. These weren't proposals being declined. They were projects with staff hired, patients enrolled, and years of work behind them.

What this chart can't say. 123 of the 1,249 grants are left out because their recorded dates don't support the calculation. The largest group, with 71 grants, had their project end date updated to the termination date, which makes a grant look 100% finished no matter how far along it actually was.

5.3 Which grants, and whose (808 grants)

Money and time together. Each bar is one year of funding; the height is the total already spent on grants that far along. Use the dropdowns to pick out a region, a type of institution, or a research area.

The money is concentrated in grants that had been running for years. A first-year grant that gets cut costs the public a few tens of thousands of dollars; a fifth-year grant costs well over a million. Whichever group you highlight, the shape holds: the cuts landed on mature work.

What this chart can't say. It shows 808 of the 1,249 grants (those with both a funding length and a spending amount on record). It leaves out one record with a negative spending figure (an accounting artifact) and one unusually large $48 million award that would flatten everything else. Both are still counted in the dollar totals above.

Sources and denominators. Grant Witness (grant status, award and spending figures, sourced from USAspending) merged with NIH RePORTER (funding history, institution, research area) on core award number: 5,757 grants, of which 1,249 were terminated and not restored.

Conclusion

The NIH is experiencing a time of unprecedented defunding

This analysis summarizes key patterns in NIH grant disruptions:

  • Funding disruptions notably accelerated
  • Termination rates varied by region
  • Private institutions had their funding reinstated faster
  • Public health research was disproportionately affected
  • Many terminated grants were already well underway

Overall, our findings suggest that the cuts were not random, but instead followed systemic patterns which disproportionately affected the industry.
